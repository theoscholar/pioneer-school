Superiority of the Translation · why we trust it

Heart of it: The New World Translation is accurate, clear, and honest — it puts God's name back where it belongs and uses the best available manuscripts.

2 Timothy 2:15 — a workman "handling the word of the truth aright."

QWhy was there a need for a new translation?

Older translations often used archaic language, reflected doctrinal bias, and hid God's name. A fresh, accurate translation was needed to help honest-hearted people understand the truth.

🔍Look it up: w15 12/15 p. 8, pars. 16-17

QHow long did it take to complete, and how have people responded?

The complete New World Translation took about 12 years of careful work (released in parts 1950–1960). Around the world, people are deeply moved to read the Bible clearly in their own language.

🔍Look it up: kr p. 39, par. 1·kr p. 84, par. 16

QWhy especially is it superior to other translations?

It restores God's name, Jehovah, thousands of times, translates accurately from the best texts, and stays faithful to the original meaning rather than to tradition.

🔍Look it up: kr p. 39, par. 2

QWhy a revised edition — and what was the committee's goal?

Language changes over time. The 2013 revision aimed for modern, easy-to-understand wording that stays accurate — making it even easier to read and share.

🔍Look it up: nwt p. 39

How We Got the Bible · Appendix A1–A3

Heart of it: A good translation carries the original meaning, not just word-for-word swaps — and the text itself has been preserved with remarkable accuracy.

QWhy isn't a strict word-for-word translation always the most accurate? (A1)

Languages differ in grammar and idiom. A literal swap can be confusing or wrong — a reliable translation conveys the correct idea, without doctrinal bias, and keeps God's name.

🔍Look it up: nwt pp. 1718-1721

QWhat kinds of changes were made in the revision? (A2)

Modern understandable language, Bible expressions clarified, better readability, the correct sense of gendered words, and dropping awkward markers for "you (plural)."

🔍Look it up: nwt pp. 1722-1725

QHow did the Bible come down to us so reliably? (A3)

Manuscripts were copied by hand for centuries. Careful copyists — the Sopherim and later the Masoretes — guarded the Hebrew text. The Leningrad Codex is the oldest complete Hebrew manuscript, and comparing the Dead Sea Scrolls with it confirms the text was faithfully preserved.

Small copy differences don't shake our faith — they're minor, and no teaching depends on a doubtful verse. Spurious verses (like the long ending of Mark 16 and John 7:53–8:11) were left out or footnoted for honesty.

🔍Look it up: nwt pp. 1726-1730

The Divine Name · Appendix A4–A5

Heart of it: God has a name — Jehovah — and it belongs in the Bible, in both the Hebrew and the Greek Scriptures.

QWhy is God's name missing from many translations — and why does the NWT use "Jehovah"? (A4)

Superstition and tradition led translators to replace the name with titles like "LORD." But the Tetragrammaton (YHWH) appears about 7,000 times in the Hebrew text. "Jehovah" is the most familiar English form, so the NWT restores it.

🔍Look it up: nwt pp. 1731-1735

QWhat does the name Jehovah mean?

It means "He Causes to Become" — Jehovah becomes whatever is needed to fulfil his purpose. Its meaning isn't limited to the words at Exodus 3:14; he can become anything required to accomplish his will.

🔍Look it up: Ex. 3:14·nwt p. 1735

QWhy does the name appear in the Christian Greek Scriptures too? (A5)

Where the Greek text quotes the Hebrew Scriptures that contained the divine name, the NWT restores it — appearing 237 times. There's solid evidence the name was in the original writings, and it helps readers see Jehovah clearly in the "New Testament" too.

🔍Look it up: nwt pp. 1736-1743
